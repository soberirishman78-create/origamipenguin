A Brief History of Origami

Origami's story stretches back centuries and spans continents, evolving from a ceremonial craft into one of the most recognizable paper arts in the world.

Early Origins in Japan

Paper folding in Japan is believed to date back to at least the 600s, following the introduction of paper from China. Early folded paper was used in Shinto ceremonies, including folded paper strips called shide still seen at shrines today. Because paper was expensive and precious, early origami was reserved for formal and ceremonial occasions rather than everyday play.

From Ceremony to Craft

By the Edo period (1603 to 1868), paper had become more affordable, and origami spread as a recreational pastime. Traditional models like the crane and simple boxes were passed down through families, often taught to children as a way to develop patience and fine motor skills.

Origami Reaches the World

In the 20th century, artist Akira Yoshizawa is widely credited with transforming origami into a respected art form. He developed the wet-folding technique for sculptural curves and created a system of diagram notation, dots, arrows, and dashed lines, that's still the international standard used in tutorials today, including the ones on this site.

Origami Today

Modern origami now stretches from simple beginner models to complex mathematical tessellations, and even influences fields like aerospace engineering and medical stent design. Despite all that complexity, the appeal for most folders is still the same as it was centuries ago: turning a single flat sheet of paper into something new using nothing but your hands.

Fold a Piece of History

The crane is the most iconic traditional model you can fold today — the same design passed down through Edo-period families. If you're brand new, start with the Beginner's Guide to learn Yoshizawa's diagram symbols first.
