When the source is missing, the review has a boundary

One curriculum review could inspect a listing and sample, but not the full manuscript. Those are different kinds of evidence.

Note 50 of 52PublishingOrigami PenguinPublished November 4, 20262 min read

The KDP curriculum work encountered a source problem: the full current manuscript was not available in the reviewed files. The listing and a limited sample could still be examined, but they could not support a verdict about every activity inside the book.

That situation is inconvenient, especially when the next improvement feels urgent. It is also a clear boundary. You can identify questions raised by the sample. You can propose a coverage inventory. You cannot truthfully report that missing pages have been checked.

The useful response is to separate what you know from what you need next. Which claim can be verified now? Which correction is a draft suggestion? Which decision depends on obtaining the complete source?

This approach keeps a blocked task from becoming either an invented success or a total waste of time. There may be valuable preparation to do while access is unresolved. The preparation should keep its correct label.

For your own review, begin by identifying the source version. If the file is incomplete, say where the evidence stops. Put proposed revisions somewhere they cannot be confused with changes already applied to the live product.

There is no creative advantage in pretending a missing file is present. A precise checkpoint is more useful than an overconfident conclusion. It tells the next session where to begin and protects the reader from a promise the work has not yet earned.
